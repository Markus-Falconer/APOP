1 · How the rotor makes voltage

Cross-section to scale, looking at the engine from the front. Slowed from rpm for viewing.

0°

The diametric magnet (N half dark, S half light) spins with the shaft. The arrows in the gap show the radial field the winding sees: outward over the N half, inward over the S half. That pattern sweeps past the six coil bands once per revolution.

By Faraday's law each phase's voltage is its rate of change of flux linkage:

e = N·dλ/dt → Epk = ω · N · kw · 2·rw·L · Bw

Voltage scales with speed ω, turns N, winding radius rw, active length L, and the field at the winding Bw. A 2-pole rotor makes one electrical cycle per revolution, so the frequency is rpm ÷ 60.

2 · Three-phase voltage and the rectifier

The three phases are 120° apart. A six-diode bridge connects whichever line-to-line pair is highest, so the DC side sees the top of the line-to-line envelope. It ripples between 87% and 100% of the peak, six times per cycle.

Vdc ≈ (3√2/π)·VLL,rms = 1.35·VLL,rms, VLL,rms = √3·Epk/√2

Turns per phase are picked so the open-circuit voltage matches the DC bus. Fewer turns means more current. Turns change the voltage and current, but not the power: power is set by the magnet, the copper, and the speed.

3 · Why magnet radius matters

A 2-pole magnet's field falls off roughly as 1/r² outside its surface. The stator back-iron at radius Ry adds an image term that holds it up near the yoke:

B(r) ≈ (Br/2)·rm2·(1/r2 + 1/Ry2)·kcore

The sleeve, clearance, and winding together add about 4 mm of non-magnetic gap. Beside a 5 mm magnet that gap is large and much of the field is lost. Beside a 10 mm magnet it is much smaller. That is why small rotors need a much longer active length, as the chart in section 5 shows.

4 · Torque, power, and speed

Torque comes from the magnetic shear stress between the rotor field and the winding's current sheet K:

σ = ½·Bw·K, K = kd·√2·J·tw·kfill, T = 2π·rw2·L·σ, P = T·ω

Holding the current density J fixed holds the torque fixed, so power rises in proportion to speed. Windage rises with ω³ and bites at the top end. The 1 kW goal is easiest at full speed.

5 · Active length vs magnet radius

For a fixed power target, torque is fixed. A small magnet has to make up for its weak field and small lever arm with length. The rotor's mass stays fairly flat across radii, but a longer rotor sits further from the front bearing. That increases the overhung inertia, which is the main rotordynamics risk.

6 · Where the shaft power goes

Pcu = ρcu·J2·Vcu Pwind ≈ π·Cf·ρair·ω3·r4·L

"Other" covers rotor eddy currents, back-iron loss, and AC (skin and proximity) loss in the winding at about 2.3 kHz. These are hard to estimate by hand, so it is a percentage input until FEA replaces it. Rotor losses are the dangerous ones: they heat a small magnet that has little cooling.

Design summary

Every value updates with the inputs. Stresses use the maximum speed input.

Rotor stress. Centrifugal hoop stress in a spinning ring is σθ = ((3+ν)/4)·ρ·ω²·(ro² + ((1−ν)/(3+ν))·ri²). Sintered magnets are weak in tension, at tens of MPa. The carbon sleeve has to be shrunk on hard enough to keep the magnet in compression at top speed. The sleeve then carries the magnet's centrifugal load:

p = ρm·ω2·(rm3 − ri3) / (3·rm), σsleeve ≈ p·rs/ts + ρs·ω2·rs2

Design options A and B

Both options use a diametric 2-pole ring magnet on a steel nut-rotor (1/4" left-hand thread) that replaces the front compressor nut, inside a slotless litz stator. Each is sized by this page's model for 1.1 kW DC (10% over the 1 kW target), with the stator at 20 A/mm² for short duty. The specs are computed live, so they stay in step with the model. Treat them as ±30% until FEA. Click a column's button to load that design into the calculator.

Recommendation: Option A. It has the same rotor mass as B, but the rotor is shorter and closer to the front bearing. It also fits the stock Ø36 mm nose. Pick SmCo or NdFeB 42SH from the dummy-rotor temperature test, and keep B as the fallback if sleeve stress or rotordynamics look bad.

Before buying magnets: run a steel dummy nut-rotor with the same mass and centroid ($150–400). Fit an accelerometer on the housing and put temperature lacquer on the nut.

Model notes and assumptions

  • An analytical slotless model with an ideal 2-pole diametric magnet. The field uses the 2D solution with an infinitely permeable back-iron. A non-magnetic (titanium) core scales the flux by 1 − (ri/rm)². A steel core acts as a partial flux return, estimated at 1 − 0.2·(ri/rm)².
  • Torque is divided by the derating factor (default 1.3) to cover end effects, armature reaction, and rectifier current distortion. "Other losses" (default 12% of electromagnetic power) stands in for rotor eddy, back-iron, and AC copper losses.
  • Copper resistivity is taken at about 100 °C. End turns add 20 mm of copper length (10 mm each end), and the stator is 16 mm longer than the active length. Windage uses a constant skin-friction coefficient Cf = 0.008.
  • Rotor mass includes the magnet, the carbon sleeve, and the threaded core (bore to the 1/4" thread, plus 8 mm for the flats and seat). Added mass is net of the stock 4 g nut. The 220 g total rotor and the 54 g compressor are your measurements.
  • Overhung inertia is mass × (distance from the front bearing)², compared with the compressor's 54 g at about 15 mm. The magnet is assumed to start 5 mm ahead of the compressor's front face, and the compressor height is 29 mm.
  • Compressor power is a rough scaling of 33 kW × (rpm / 155,000)³, from the datasheet pressure ratio and mass flow. For thrust and EGT effects, enter this page's DC power and efficiency as "Electrical power out" and "Generator + rectifier efficiency" on the Cycle analysis V2 page.
  • Voltages are open-circuit. Under load, the winding resistance and inductance and the diode drops pull the DC bus down by a few percent.
  • Costs are prototype-quantity estimates, not quotes.
