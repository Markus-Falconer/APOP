The idea

Put a second turbine wheel, a copy of the engine's own, on its own shaft behind the engine. It drives the generator, and a new nozzle encloses it and converges the flow. The core engine is untouched mechanically; the two are coupled only through the gas. This page works out what that costs the engine, how fast the spare wheel would spin, and what the hardware has to survive.

1. Layout

Station 4 is the core turbine exit, where the ECU thermocouple sits. Station 45 is the aft turbine exit, and station 5 is the new nozzle exit. The aft wheel needs its own bearings, held in a hub on struts across the hot gas, and the generator has to sit in or next to that hub. The core turbine still drives only the compressor, so the ECU keeps holding corrected speed exactly as it does now.

3. The aft rotor: Euler equation and velocity triangles

A turbine's work per kilogram comes from how much it changes the gas's swirl (tangential velocity Vθ) at blade speed U:

w = U·(Vθ1 − Vθ2)    Vθ1 = Vx·tan α₁    Vθ2 = U − Vx·tan β₂

α₁ is the swirl angle of the gas arriving at the wheel (0 with no stator, if the core turbine leaves the gas swirl-free). β₂ is the rotor blade's exit angle, set by the wheel's geometry. Combining them:

w = U·(C − U)  with  C = Vx·(tan α₁ + tan β₂)    runaway: U = C    peak: w = C²/4 at U = C/2

Incidence: the copy wheel sees the wrong inflow

In the engine, the guide vanes swirl the gas so it meets the rotor blades nearly head-on. Behind the engine there is no such swirl, and the gas meets the blades at about from axial. That is roughly off the direction the blades were designed for. That incidence is why the effective efficiency of the copy wheel back here is likely low (the 0.5 default is a placeholder until the loaded tests measure it).

A stator that pre-swirls the gas would fix the incidence, but it also raises C, and with it the runaway speed. Try "Inflow swirl α₁" on the left. Even 20–30° pushes the runaway well past the wheel's rated speed. For 1 kW, the copy wheel is a better fit without a stator. A custom wheel would instead use much less blade turning, which lowers both incidence and runaway speed.

4. Why the spare NGV can't be reused

The engine's nozzle guide vanes are choked, so their throat area sets how much gas they pass at a given inlet pressure and temperature:

ṁ = A* · P₀ / √T₀ · √(γ/R) · (2/(γ+1))(γ+1)/(2(γ−1))

The calibration's NGV flow parameter gives a core throat area of about . Behind the engine, pressure is about of the core turbine inlet's, so even a choked aft stator would need at least of throat, the core's. A copy NGV would pass only about half the flow. Pressure would back up behind the core turbine until the engine surged or over-temperatured.

So the prototype uses the spare rotor only, with no stator, or with thin struts aligned to the flow. Any future stator needs its own design with a much larger throat area.

5. Effect on the engine

At 90% corrected speed the ECU holds speed. The aft turbine plus its duct and struts take total pressure out of the flow, so the nozzle needs a higher turbine-exit pressure to pass the same gas. That is the same chain as shrinking the nozzle: more fuel and a higher EGT. Taking power off the front of the core shaft instead loads the core turbine directly. 8. Prototype plan with the spare wheel

  1. Measure the spare wheel.
    • Tip and hub diameter, blade count, axial chord (6.4 mm) and mass.
    • Blade inlet and exit angles at mid-span: photograph the blade edge-on against a protractor or use a contour gauge. β₂ sets the runaway speed, so this is the most important number on this page.
    • Check the wheel's balance and condition, and how it was mounted (bore, nut, axial location).
  2. Design the aft module.
    • A hub on 3–4 thin struts aligned with the flow, carrying a bearing cartridge, the wheel and the generator rotor.
    • A housing around the wheel with a tip clearance similar to the core's, then a converging nozzle to the exit diameter from section 5.
    • Instrumentation provisions: rotor speed (optical or magnetic pickup), bearing and generator temperature, and total-pressure probes at stations 4 and 45.
    • Containment: the housing must hold a burst wheel (see the stored energy in section 6).
  3. Balance the rotor assembly (wheel, shaft and generator rotor) and spin it on a bench, if you can, before it goes behind the engine.
  4. Windmill test, low speeds first.
    • With a small fixed load or brake always on, never open-circuit, run the engine at idle, 50% and 70% and log aft rotor rpm against engine rpm.
    • Extrapolate to full throttle. If the projected runaway exceeds the wheel's rating, stop and add load or redesign before going higher.
    • This measures C, the effective blade angle.
  5. Loaded test. Step electrical load at 90% corrected speed while logging power, aft rpm, EGT, thrust and both pressure probes. That gives the real aft-turbine efficiency and duct loss; enter them on the left.
  6. Compare with the front-shaft option on equal terms: EGT and thrust at 90% + 1 kW, full-throttle EGT, length, weight and risk (section 7).
  7. Decide on a custom turbine only if the copy wheel shows the layout works:
    • 1 kW at 90% with EGT margin.
    • Runaway under control.
    • A generator that survives the heat.
    • A total score at least as good as the front-shaft option.
    A custom wheel would use far less blade turning, sized for about 5–10 kJ/kg at a chosen speed, plus a larger flow area or a matched stator. That cuts the back-pressure, the incidence loss and the runaway speed.
The spare wheel is designed to deliver around 30 kW to the compressor. Asking it for about 1 kW is like using a truck engine at idle: it can do it, but it sits far from its design point. Most of the risks on this page come from that mismatch, and most go away with a wheel designed for the job.
