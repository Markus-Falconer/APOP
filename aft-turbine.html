The idea

A second turbine rotor on its own shaft behind the engine, inside a new housing that ends in a converging nozzle. A gear train carries its power out through a strut to an off-the-shelf brushless motor, used as a generator and mounted outside the exhaust with insulation and active cooling. The core engine is untouched mechanically; the two are coupled only through the gas. Two rotor options are compared: the spare core wheel for early prototyping, and a low-cost rotor designed for 1 kW.

1. Layout

Station 4 is the core turbine exit, where the ECU thermocouple sits. Station 45 is the aft rotor exit, and station 5 is the new nozzle exit. The rotor's bearings and a bevel pinion sit in a hub held on struts in the hot gas. A cross shaft runs out through one hollow strut to a gearbox and the generator outside the housing, where insulation and a cooling flow keep it within its temperature rating. The core turbine still drives only the compressor, so the ECU keeps holding corrected speed exactly as it does now.

3. Rotor physics: Euler equation and velocity triangles

A turbine's work per kilogram comes from how much it changes the gas's swirl (tangential velocity Vθ) at blade speed U:

w = U·(Vθ1 − Vθ2)    Vθ1 = Vx·tan α₁    Vθ2 = U − Vx·tan β₂

α₁ is the swirl angle of the gas arriving at the rotor (0 with no stator, if the core turbine leaves the gas swirl-free). β₂ is the rotor blade's exit angle. Combining them:

w = U·(C − U)  with  C = Vx·(tan α₁ + tan β₂)    runaway: U = C    peak: w = C²/4 at U = C/2

Incidence

The gas meets the selected rotor's blades at about from axial, roughly off the direction they were designed for. The spare wheel's blades expect gas that the core's guide vanes have already swirled. Behind the engine there is no swirl, so it runs at large incidence, which is why its effective efficiency here is likely low (0.5 is a placeholder until a loaded test measures it). A stator that pre-swirls the gas would fix the incidence, but it raises C and the runaway speed with it; try "Inflow swirl α₁". The low-cost rotor in section 4 is designed for swirl-free inflow instead.

4. A low-cost rotor for 1 kW

The spare wheel was built to give about 30 kW to the compressor, and at 1 kW it spins near its free-running speed with large incidence. A rotor designed for the job can turn more slowly, be cheaper to make, and take the gas head-on. Pick a design speed Nd at the 90% + load condition; with no stator and swirl-free inflow:

Ud = π·Dm·Nd/60    C = Ud + w/Ud    tan β₂ = C/Vx    tan β₁ = Ud/Vx  (blade inlet angle for zero incidence)

The blades only need to turn the flow a few degrees, so they are close to flat plates set at a stagger angle, like a windmill. That makes cheap construction possible:

  • Laser-cut sheet. A disk of Inconel 625 sheet (about 1 mm) with blade tabs cut around the rim, each tab twisted to the stagger angle in a jig, then welded or brazed to a turned hub. Cheapest; balance carefully.
  • 3D-printed metal. A one-piece wheel in Inconel 718 or 625 from an online metal printing service, with slightly cambered blades, then turned on the bore and tip and balanced. More consistent, and still far cheaper than a custom-machined turbine.

Choose a nickel alloy either way: the rotor sits in gas at about 650–730 °C, and common stainless steels lose most of their strength there. The design speed trades three things against each other. Higher speed gives more power margin and less torque through the gears, but raises the runaway speed and blade stress. Lower speed is safer and easier to gear, but leaves less margin if the blades turn the flow less than intended. 55,000 rpm balances them with the default inputs: about 1.4× power margin and a blade safety factor of about 1.6 at full-throttle runaway. Thin flat blades can also flutter, so check the blade's first natural frequency against the rotor speed and the passing frequency of the struts upstream.

5. Why the spare NGV can't be reused

The engine's nozzle guide vanes are choked, so their throat area sets how much gas they pass at a given inlet pressure and temperature:

ṁ = A* · P₀ / √T₀ · √(γ/R) · (2/(γ+1))(γ+1)/(2(γ−1))

The calibration's NGV flow parameter gives a core throat of about . Behind the engine, pressure is only about of the core turbine inlet's, so even a choked aft stator would need at least of throat, the core's. A copy NGV would pass only about half the flow. Pressure would back up behind the core turbine until the engine surged or over-temperatured. Either rotor runs without a stator, behind thin struts aligned with the flow.

6. Gear train and generator

Power flows from the rotor through the gears and the motor to the load bank, losing a little at each step:

Protor = (Pelec + Pcooling) / (ηgen·ηgear) + Pbearings    Ngen = Nrotor / i    vpitch = π·dpinion·Nrotor/60
  • First stage in the hub. A bevel pinion on the rotor shaft turns the power 90° into a cross shaft through a hollow strut. Pitch-line speeds of 25–60 m/s in a hot hub call for precision-ground gears, a lubrication path and a heat path out. The low-cost rotor's lower speed roughly halves the gear speed.
  • Generator outside. A hobby brushless motor run as a generator, with a 3-phase rectifier to 1–200 VDC. Choose its Kv so the rectified voltage lands where you want it, roughly Kv ≈ generator rpm ÷ target volts (the table uses 60 V). It must also survive the runaway generator speed, not just the operating speed.
  • Insulation and active cooling. The motor sits next to a housing carrying 650–730 °C gas. Hobby motors' magnets and windings are typically rated for about 100–150 °C, so use a heat shield or air gap, a low-conductivity mount, and a fan or compressed-air flow. A fan powered from the generator comes out of the delivered power; enter it as "Cooling power". A fan or air supply in the support box is support equipment and must be approved at the Milestone 4 review.
  • Radial size. A motor beside the housing sticks out past the engine case, and radial distance is scored (15 points at 0 in, −3.75 per inch). Keep the motor tucked close, or align it behind the engine.

7. Effect on the engine

At 90% corrected speed the ECU holds speed. The rotor plus its duct and struts take total pressure out of the flow, so the nozzle needs a higher turbine-exit pressure to pass the same gas. That is the same chain as shrinking the nozzle: more fuel and a higher EGT. Taking power off the front of the core shaft instead loads the core turbine directly. 10. Prototype plan

  1. Measure what you have. On the spare wheel, record tip and hub diameter, blade count, and the blade exit angle at mid-span: photograph the blade edge-on against a protractor. β₂ sets the spare's runaway speed. Also confirm the turbine-exit hub diameter on the engine, which sets the flow area and speed into the rotor.
  2. Build and bench-test the drivetrain first. Assemble the hub bearings, bevel stage, cross shaft, external motor and rectifier. Drive the input with an electric spindle or air turbine up to the rotor's runaway speed. Measure gear and bearing temperatures, losses and generator output into a resistor. This de-risks the hardest mechanical parts away from the engine.
  3. Choose the rotor. Use the spare wheel to prove the layout cheaply, or go straight to the low-cost rotor (section 4) if the spare's speed and gear speeds look too high for your drivetrain. Balance the rotor, shaft and pinion together.
  4. Windmill test, low engine speeds first. Always keep a load connected: a resistor bank with an overspeed trip, never open-circuit. Run at idle, 50% and 70%, and log rotor rpm against engine rpm. Extrapolate to full throttle and stop if the projected runaway exceeds the rotor's or the generator's limit. This measures C, the effective blade angle.
  5. Loaded test at 90% corrected speed. Step the electrical load up to 1 kW while logging power, rotor rpm, EGT, thrust, generator temperature and total pressure at stations 4 and 45. That gives the real rotor efficiency and duct loss; enter them on the left.
  6. Compare with the front-shaft option on equal terms: EGT and thrust at 90% + 1 kW, full-throttle EGT, length, radial size, weight, cost and risk (section 9).
  7. Decide on a custom turbine only if the aft layout works. That means 1 kW at 90% with EGT margin, a controlled runaway, a cool enough generator, and a total score at least as good as the front-shaft option. The low-cost rotor may already be enough. A properly designed custom wheel would add a matched stator and a larger, slower flow area to cut back-pressure further.
