Goal. Couple the Lehner 1525 starter-generator's 5 mm front shaft to the P100 compressor nut with no contact, so misalignment and vibration on one shaft do not reach the other, while carrying the torque for 1,000 W DC with a good margin. The motor turns at engine speed: about 141,000–144,000 rpm while generating, a proposed 145,000 rpm cap, 154,000 rpm at maximum and 160,000 rpm on test.

Verdict

Use a 6-pole coaxial (radial-gap) synchronous magnet coupler. A light inner rotor of six SmCo arcs under a titanium sleeve goes on the motor shaft. A steel cup lined with six N42UH arcs goes on the compressor nut.

  • Torque. Pull-out is about 0.25 N·m cold and 0.22 N·m hot, against 84 mN·m nominal and 103 mN·m worst-case running torque: at least 2.1× margin when hot.
  • Shaft-to-shaft disturbance is close to zero. No contact, no net axial force, a radial pull of about 17 N/mm of offset (about 0.2 % of the motor's bearing stiffness), and no loss while it runs in sync.
  • Motor side, about 4 g. The 1525's first mode stays at 177,000 rpm at 10 N/µm. The margin has a cliff: 5 g at 12 mm drops it to 166,000 (section 6).
  • Engine side, about 19–22 g on the nut, is the main new risk. The engine's own vibration margin is unknown, so survey and field-balance the engine with the cup fitted before running the generator.
  • Engine acceleration is not a coupler limit. The limits are electrical: load-bank mode, how the load is applied, and faults (section 9).

4. Field checks: back iron and demagnetisation

The model treats iron as ideal, so torque does not fall when steel saturates. The back-iron and shaft checks stop that assumption from hiding a problem (5.1). The demagnetising field at the gap-facing pole faces is worst when the coupler slips and like poles pass each other (9.1).

CheckValueLimitStatus

Demagnetisation limit taken as 0.9 × Hcj at the magnet temperature (the knee of a square loop). Parallel-magnetised arcs push more flux into the cup than radial ones, so they need a slightly thicker wall for the same torque.

5. Shaft-to-shaft disturbance

PathValueWhat it means
  • Unbalance is not passed across. Each half is balanced with its own rotor; the coupler only couples them through a weak magnetic spring.
  • Stray field. The open end leaks the rotating pole field into nearby stationary aluminium. With 6 poles at about 143,700 rpm that field alternates at about 7.2 kHz, where aluminium's skin depth is about 1 mm. A rough estimate is a few watts at 4–5 mm and about 10 W at 2–3 mm, heat that lands in the end bell next to the bearing. Keep a 4 mm standoff from the end bell, fit a 0.8 mm steel shield washer on the shaft, keep at least 5 mm to any aluminium bracket (or make that part from G10 or PEEK), and confirm with a spin-down.

Two modes compete. Up to a threshold mass the first mode is set by the rotor itself and does not move; above it, the overhung coupler mode drops below it and falls quickly. Keep the half below the knee and its centroid within about 10 mm of the front-bearing centre. A 15 g cup on the motor side would drop the mode to about 128,000 rpm, which is why the cup goes on the engine.

7. Engine side: mass and balance

ItemValueNotes
  • The engine's vibration margin with extra mass at the nut is unknown. JetCat does hard-couple a no-contact 900 W generator to the shaft of its P300 PRO-GH, so there is precedent, but the P100 rotor is smaller.
  • Field-balance the engine in place with the cup fitted: cup rim as the correction plane, the generator's phase voltage as the once-per-rev reference (the motor is 2-pole), and an index mark so the cup goes back on in the same position.
  • Survey vibration from idle to the 145,000 rpm cap with the generator open-circuit, and compare with the stock baseline before any loaded run.

8. Load-application simulator

The motor rotor rides on the coupler spring against the much heavier engine rotor, which is held at the generating speed (13.1). Pick how the load comes on and watch the coupler angle. Crossing 180° electrical means a pole slipped; the coupler does not re-lock at speed.

Coupler anglePull-out angleSlip (180° electrical)

Uses the hot torque-angle curve and the motor-side inertia from the left panel. A step overshoots to about twice the static angle because almost nothing damps the coupler: a constant-resistance load adds a damping ratio of only a few thousandths, constant current adds none, and a constant-power load that follows a 140 Hz oscillation adds negative damping. Whether a real load bank does follow it depends on its control loop: try a 5 ms lag. Treat the CP result as a risk, not a prediction.

9. Operating procedures

What the coupler does not need

  • No special acceleration limit.
  • No overspeed hazard. The generator rides on the ECU-governed spool, unlike the aft-turbine concepts.

Run sequence (follows the scoring test sequence)

  1. Before every run. Light-check the coupler gap for ferrous debris: the magnets collect it in the intake (Tinker is FOD-critical). Clean it with tape and use non-magnetic tools near the intake. Turn the engine by hand: motor and engine should turn together with no rub. Check the torque stripes on the nut and cup.
  2. Set up the electrics. Load contactor open. Load bank in constant resistance for about 1 kW at 90 %: R ≈ Vdc²/P with the measured loaded DC voltage. ESC current limit at most half the measured pull-out torque:
  3. Start. If the motor's speed runs away from the engine speed the ECU reports, the coupler slipped: abort. Disarm the ESC and open the phase relay before the back-EMF passes the ESC bus voltage (about 70,000 rpm for the /17 on 12S), or its body diodes brake the motor hard and slip the coupler.
  4. Go to 90 % with no load.
  5. Step 3 hold: apply the load. Wait for rpm and EGT to settle, close the contactor, and ramp the load in over about 3–5 s (high resistance first, or the load bank's slew setting). The ramp is for the engine: a step makes the ECU see an rpm droop and over-fuel, so EGT spikes. Watch EGT (ICD limit about 800 °C), DC volts and amps, and the slip check. Reach steady state before the 1-minute hold.
  6. Steps 4–5: hands off. Constant resistance is stable.
  7. Step 6: ramp the load to zero before the max-thrust acceleration. Power is not scored there. Load during the acceleration raises EGT and costs max thrust.
  8. Steps 7–9: the motor runs open-circuit. Keep the open-circuit voltage under the 200 V ICD limit at the top speed actually reached (delta /17: about 107 V at 154,000 rpm).
  9. Shutdown and cool-down. The ECU spins the engine with the starter at a constant speed after shutdown; here that is the ESC driving the motor, at low torque. Cool-down also limits soak-back into the nut magnets.

If it slips

  • Detect: the generator's electrical frequency × 60 no longer matches the ECU rpm, or the DC voltage collapses while the engine holds speed, or a buzz is heard. Automate it in the support box: open the load contactor if the speeds disagree by more than about 2 % for more than about 50 ms, and flag the control room.
  • Respond: load off, engine to idle, then shut down. Running slipped vibrates the coupler and heats the magnets. Inspect and repeat the torque check before the next run.
  • The coupler is the system's torque fuse. Things that can slip it: a short in the cable or rectifier, the ESC body-diode trap, CV mode, too high a starter current limit, a large load step when hot, or magnet and sleeve damage. A modelling study of a diesel generator set found the same: a magnetic coupling cut the peak torque on the shafts in a severe fault, at the cost of slower resynchronisation.

Returning to stock

With the cup on the nut the stock O-ring starter cannot grip the nut. Plan the fallback as generator disconnected, motor used only as the starter, and carry the stock starter and bracket in case the cup must come off. Removing and refitting the cup changes the engine balance.

10. Tests before engine runs

  1. Static torque–angle curve. Clamp the cup, turn the inner half with a lever arm and force gauge through pull-out, cold and then at about 100 °C. Accept at least 0.22 N·m cold and 0.18 N·m hot. This calibrates the end factor. Repeat after a deliberate pull-out to check for demagnetisation.
  2. Spin rig stage 2 (already planned): drive through the coupler, coast-down loss with and without it, end-bell temperature rise, and a tap test with the inner half fitted.
  3. Engine with the cup, generator open-circuit: vibration survey to the 145,000 rpm cap against the stock baseline, field balance, and a temperature label on the nut to capture soak-back after shutdown.
  4. Start the engine through the coupler with the ESC. A sensorless start against a 140 Hz elastic link can lose sync; tune the start ramp and current limit.

11. Build notes

  • Buy: custom 60° arcs from a magnet vendor, six per ring plus spares, parallel magnetised, ±0.02 mm on the radii, coated. Order early: lead time is weeks. Catalogue diametric rings only suit the rejected 2-pole layout.
  • Inner rotor: bond the arcs to the degreased shaft with alternating polarity in a printed 6-slot fixture (Loctite 648 or a high-temperature epoxy). Check polarity with a Hall probe or viewing film. Shrink-fit the titanium sleeve by heating it to about 250 °C, which SmCo tolerates, for about 15–20 µm interference. Balance the rotor with the half fitted to G1, at Lehner or by field balancing on the spin rig.
  • Outer cup: bond the arcs into the back-iron ring with alternating polarity and fit the retaining lip. At speed the arcs are thrown outward onto the iron, so they need no sleeve. The hub pilots on the nut's 13.25 mm round land and drives through the 11 mm flats. Measure the nut first: land diameter and runout, flat length, thread hand and tightening torque. Generator torque acts opposite to starter torque on the nut, so torque-stripe the nut and cup and check them every run.
  • Alignment: turned spigots on the motor bracket. Indicate the cup rim and the sleeve: concentricity at or under 0.1 mm, angle at or under 0.3°, inner half centred axially in the longer outer ring.
  • Assembly safety: the radial pull near contact is tens of newtons. Fit the motor on guide pins with a Mylar shim, never let the inner half snap against the cup, and mind the pinch hazard.

Model notes and assumptions

  • Torque (2.1)–(4.3): 2-D magnetic-charge model. Magnets have relative permeability 1, iron is ideal at the shaft surface and the cup bore, and each angular harmonic (odd multiples of p up to 9p) is solved exactly with the Green's function between the two iron cylinders. It matches the planar shear-stress formula to 0.1 % at large radius. 3-D end leakage is one factor, 0.85 by default; a 3-D charge model of an 8 mm long coupler gave 0.87. Torque scales with the overlap of the two rings, so an axial offset only costs torque once it exceeds half the extra length of the outer ring.
  • Back iron (5.1): the fundamental flux entering the cup is carried around the wall at the chosen flux density. Saturation is screened, not modelled.
  • Radial pull (6.1): planar two-array energy at the gap radius, differentiated with respect to the gap.
  • Retention (8.1)–(8.2): magnets with no hoop strength pressing on thin rings, at the test speed.
  • Motor modes (11.1): the coupler half is spread over its length as four masses with rotary inertia; the shield washer adds 0.5 g. The shaft extension is set to standoff + inner length + 2 mm. No gyroscopic stiffening, so the figures are slightly conservative.
  • Simulator (13.1): the engine rotor is about 30× the motor rotor's inertia, so it is held at constant speed. No other damping unless entered.
  • Superseded figures. The "Direct coaxial coupler" column on the Magnetic gear options page is an earlier concept for the 1535 at 85,000 rpm: a 13 g half on the motor shaft and a decoupling actuator for max thrust. The 1525 runs at engine speed, so no decoupler is needed and the light half goes on the motor.
