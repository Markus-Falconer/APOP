1. Nozzle physics

What the nozzle does

The turbine leaves the gas above ambient pressure. The nozzle turns that leftover pressure into jet velocity. On a static thrust stand there is no ram drag, so thrust is the jet momentum plus any pressure difference left at the exit:

F = ṁ·V₅ + (P₅ − Pamb)·A₅
V₅ = √( 2·ηn·cp·T₀₄·[1 − (P₅ / P₀₄)(γ−1)/γ] )    ṁ = Cd·ρ₅·V₅·A₅

Station 4 is the turbine exit (nozzle inlet); station 5 is the nozzle exit plane. T₀₄ is what the ECU thermocouple reads as EGT.

Why the exit pressure is always ambient

A subsonic jet cannot hold a pressure difference at its exit. Pressure waves travel faster than the flow, so the surrounding air sets the exit-plane static pressure: P₅ = Pamb, and the pressure term in the thrust equation is zero. Only a choked jet (Mach 1 at the exit) can leave above ambient. Choking needs a nozzle pressure ratio of

NPR = P₀₄ / Pamb ≥ ((γ+1)/2)γ/(γ−1) ≈ 1.85  (γ ≈ 1.34 for hot products)

With the stock nozzle at 90% corrected speed and the generator load set on the left, the model gives NPR and exit Mach . That is far from choking, and every candidate in the sizing table stays subsonic too. Two consequences follow:

  • Thrust is all momentum, ṁ·V₅. To raise it at fixed speed you need a faster jet, which means more pressure and temperature at the turbine exit.
  • A converging-diverging nozzle would not help. A diverging section only adds thrust when the flow is choked with pressure left over. Here it would add length, weight and friction for nothing.

Exit area sets the engine's operating point

At the scored condition the ECU holds corrected speed, not fuel flow. Shrinking the exit area sets off a chain:

  1. Less gas can leave at the old turbine-exit pressure, so pressure backs up behind the turbine and P₀₄ rises.
  2. The turbine's expansion ratio P₀₃/P₀₄ falls, so it makes less work per kilogram at the same inlet temperature.
  3. The rotor starts to slow, and the ECU adds fuel to hold speed. Turbine inlet temperature T₀₃ rises until the turbine again drives the compressor and the generator.
  4. The hotter, higher-pressure gas leaves faster, so V₅ and thrust go up. EGT goes up with it.
  5. The turbine nozzle guide vanes are choked (ṁ·√T₀₃/P₀₃ is fixed), so hotter gas means slightly less air. The compressor moves up its speed line, toward surge.

So a smaller nozzle trades EGT margin and surge margin for thrust, paid for with more fuel. It does not create energy.

What limits how small you can go

  • EGT. The ICD says not to exceed about 800 °C and warns specifically that a smaller exit raises EGT. EGT climbs faster with area reduction at full throttle than at 90%, so the full-throttle run usually sets the limit.
  • Turbine inlet temperature. T₀₃ rises along with EGT (see the T₀₃ column in the sizing table). The turbine wheel and guide vanes see this temperature, and it is not measured.
  • Compressor surge. Each step down in area pushes the compressor toward lower flow and higher pressure ratio. Without a compressor map the model can only report how far it has moved, not where surge is. Watch for popping, rumbling, or rpm and EGT oscillating during tests.
  • Generator load. At 90% speed the model adds about of EGT per kW extracted. Size the nozzle with the generator loaded.
  • Hot test day. On a hot day, 90% corrected speed means a higher physical rpm. Set the ambient on the left to the hottest day you expect at the test site.
  • The ECU. If the ECU caps EGT or pulls rpm back near its limit, a small nozzle may stop the full-throttle run short of full speed.

Losses and contour

The flow leaves the turbine through an annulus between the outer wall and the inner (tail) cone: about , at roughly (Mach ). The stock exit is larger than that annulus, and the area behind a short tail cone is larger still (see the area plot in section 3). The flow slows down, then speeds up again. If it separates off a steep cone, it behaves like a sudden expansion. That costs roughly ½ρ(V₁ − V₂)² of total pressure, about out of the the nozzle has to work with. The real loss depends on the cone shape, which still has to be measured.

At fixed speed, a cleaner nozzle mostly lowers EGT rather than raising thrust, because the ECU then needs less fuel. Try "Nozzle efficiency gain" on the left. That margin can then be spent on a smaller exit, so contour and area work together. Guidelines for the contour:

  • Make the flow area shrink steadily from the turbine annulus to the exit, or at least avoid a large bulge. A gently tapered tail cone, or an outer wall that converges early, both help.
  • Keep the inside smooth: no steps at joints or insert edges, and a clean, round exit lip. A warped exit changes the area and can skew the jet.
  • Keep it short. Overall length is scored (full marks at 10 in, zero at 15 in), and the ICD allows at most 11.75 in behind the stand.
  • Keep the jet on the engine axis. The ICD requires the exhaust centerline within 2 in of the stock location and parallel to the facility exhaust.
  • Remember thermal growth. A stainless or Inconel exit is about 0.4–0.5 mm larger in diameter at ~650 °C than when measured cold. The calibration used the cold diameter, so stick with cold measurements and similar materials.

A variable-area option

The test sequence lets teams actuate controls before the 1-minute hold at 90% speed, and again for up to a minute before the full-throttle run. A nozzle that is small for the 90% run and opens before full throttle would remove the full-throttle EGT limit. It costs an actuator in a hot zone, weight, radial size and support equipment. A fixed nozzle sized for both runs is the simpler first step.

5. Test procedure for the 90% corrected speed condition

  1. Measure the stock nozzle properly.
    • Exit inside diameter at 4 angles (it is warped, so record the spread), inlet inside diameter, overall length and wall thickness.
    • The tail cone: base diameter, length, whether it reaches the exit plane, and its profile. A photo against a scale or a contour gauge is enough.
    • How the nozzle attaches to the engine, and the gap to the turbine wheel.
  2. Add instrumentation.
    • Thrust: calibrate the load cell with known weights before each session.
    • ECU log: rpm, EGT and pump voltage at the highest rate the ECU allows.
    • Ambient: temperature and pressure next to the intake, logged with every run.
    • Fuel flow: weigh the fuel tank before and after a timed hold. At about 5 g/s, a 60 s hold uses ~300 g, so a ±1 g scale gives better than 1%.
    • Turbine-exit total pressure (P₀₄): a small stainless tube bent to face upstream, about 1.5 mm OD, through the nozzle wall near the inlet with its tip at mid-annulus. Route it through enough tube to cool before a 0–100 kPa gauge transducer. Don't use a plain wall tap here: the flow is around Mach , so wall static pressure sits well below P₀₄ and close to ambient.
    • Optional exit-plane probe: the same kind of probe at the exit. Because P₅ = Pamb, it gives jet velocity directly. Traversing it across the exit gives the velocity profile and the discharge coefficient.
  3. Set the speed correctly. The scored condition is 90% corrected speed: physical rpm = 138,600 × √(Tamb / 288.15 K). Compute it from the intake temperature before each run.
  4. Baseline the stock nozzle at 90%.
    • Warm up, then hold the target rpm for 1 minute and average 30 s. That matches the competition sequence.
    • Then go to full throttle and average 30 s.
    • Repeat three times. The scatter tells you the smallest effect you can detect; a 0.5 mm step should change thrust by roughly 0.5–1 lbf.
    • Enter the averages as test points on Cycle analysis V2. This replaces the model's 90% extrapolation with data.
  5. Step the exit area down.
    • Make short exit rings that reduce the diameter in 0.5–0.8 mm steps (about 2–3% of area each), for example 47.6 → 46.8 → 46.0 → 45.4 mm.
    • Use stainless tube or rolled stainless shim with a tapered lead-in, not a sharp step. Clamp or screw them on; never rely on friction at temperature.
    • Run the same sequence as the baseline on each ring, at 90% and full throttle.
  6. Stop when any limit is reached.
    • EGT reaches your margin line (800 °C minus the margin) in either run.
    • Signs of surge or stall: popping, rumble, or rpm or EGT oscillating.
    • The ECU can no longer hold the target speed, or the full-throttle run stops short of full speed.
    • The thrust gain per step drops off sharply.
  7. Update the model after every ring. Enter each result on Cycle analysis V2 with its as-tested diameter. Rings at different areas pin down the compressor speed-line slope S, the main uncertainty in the sizing charts above, so each step makes the next prediction better.
  8. Run a load test at the chosen size. With the generator, or a dummy load such as a resistive load on a spare motor, hold 90% corrected speed and step electrical power up to 1 kW. Log EGT and thrust at each step to measure the EGT rise per kW directly.

6. Choosing the final nozzle

Pick the smallest exit diameter that meets all of these on the hottest expected test day with the generator at 1 kW:

  • EGT at 90% corrected speed at or below 800 °C minus your margin.
  • EGT in the full-throttle run at or below the same line. This is usually the tighter one.
  • No surge signs at any step in testing.
  • A thrust gain worth the risk. Each extra lbf at 90% is worth 2 points up to 20 lbf, while power is worth 100. A nozzle that costs a run, or the generator's output, costs far more than it gains.

Then build the final nozzle with a smooth converging contour to that exit diameter, as short as the contour allows. Check it is round and on-axis, and run the full competition sequence once more before calling it done.

The sizing charts are a starting point for testing, not a substitute for it. Close to the tested 47.62 mm nozzle the model is well anchored; each millimetre smaller leans more on the guessed compressor speed-line slope until the ring tests measure it.
