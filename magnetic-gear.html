Goal

Find out whether a magnetic gear between the engine's front shaft and the Lehner 1535 can keep the motor below its 85,000 rpm rating. That would remove the need for a 140,000 rpm overspeed rotor and a decoupling actuator. Compare a coaxial gear with a face-to-face gear, and both with the direct magnetic coupler.

Verdict. A coaxial magnetic gear works on paper. The 4/9/13 baseline, 36 mm across with 20 mm long magnets, carries the full generator load with 2.1× pull-out margin. The motor never exceeds 68,400 rpm, even at maximum thrust, so the Lehner runs inside its rating and no decoupler is needed. The costs:

  • a 42 g rotor on the compressor nut (the stock front nut is about 4 g);
  • a 77 g outer rotor that needs its own bearings;
  • about 95 W of losses;
  • a stationary pole-piece ring that must run concentric with both rotors inside 0.5 mm gaps.

A face-to-face gear is not feasible: its engine-side disc cannot hold its magnets at 154,000 rpm. Keep the direct coupler as the baseline, and treat the coaxial gear as the fallback if the Lehner fails its 140,000 rpm overspeed test, provided JetCat's rotor can take the extra mass at the nut.

1. How a magnetic gear works

A magnetic coupler locks two magnet rotors with the same number of poles, so they turn at the same speed. A magnetic gear adds a third part between them: a stationary ring of steel pole pieces separated by non-magnetic gaps. The pole pieces chop up each rotor's field. The engine rotor has a few poles, and seen through the ring its field contains a strong component with as many poles as the motor rotor. The motor rotor's field is filtered the same way. The two rotors lock to these modulated fields, so they turn at different speeds, in opposite directions, with no contact.

ns = ph + pl    nmotor = −(ph / pl)·nengine
Tmotor = −(pl / ph)·Tengine    Tring = −(Tengine + Tmotor)

ph and pl are the pole-pair counts of the high-speed (engine) and low-speed (motor) rotors, and ns is the number of pole pieces. Power is conserved apart from losses: the motor side turns slower and carries proportionally more torque. The stationary ring reacts the sum of both torques, so it needs a solid mount to the engine case.

Two layouts

  • Coaxial (radial flux). The engine rotor is a hub on the compressor nut. The pole-piece ring sits around it, and the motor rotor is a cup around that, as in the drawing. Each rotor carries magnet torque around its circumference, and the axial forces cancel when the parts are centred.
  • Face-to-face (axial flux). Engine disc, flat ring of pole pieces, motor disc, all stacked along the shaft. It is short along the shaft, but the field that makes torque also makes a large attraction: each rotor is pulled toward the steel ring.

Rules for picking pole numbers

  • pl > ph for a speed reduction. To get under 85,000 rpm at 154,000 rpm engine speed, pl/ph must be at least 1.81.
  • Few engine-side poles means long pole pitches, which push more flux through the pole pieces and back-irons. The field model shows 2 pole pairs saturating the pole pieces at this size.
  • The torque ripple from cogging depends on the factor C = 2ph·ns / LCM(2ph, ns). A value of 1 is best and usually needs an odd number of pole pieces.
  • Every pole piece sees the engine rotor's field flip at f = ph·nengine/60, about 9.3 kHz for 4 pole pairs. That frequency sets the iron and magnet losses.

2. Choosing pole numbers

Five combinations were run through the field model at 30, 34 and 38 mm outside diameter. Magnets are SmCo 30H at 120 °C (Br 1.04 T), with a 1.0 mm inner magnetic gap, a 0.5 mm outer gap and pole pieces filling half the ring. Designs were rejected where 95% of the pole-piece material exceeds 1.4 T, or the back-irons exceed 1.5 T. Torques are 2-D values per metre of magnet length, before any allowance for end effects.

Field-model check: the ratio of motor-side to engine-side peak torque matched pl/ph within 1–3% for every combination.

4/9/13 is the baseline. At 34–38 mm it gives the most torque inside the flux limits. Its cogging factor is 1, so the torque is smooth. It puts the motor at 61,800 rpm at 139,000 engine rpm and 68,400 rpm at maximum, a 20% margin under the Lehner limit. 3/8/11 is the runner-up, with a higher ratio and a slower motor (57,800 rpm at maximum), but its pole pieces run closer to saturation.

3. Coaxial gear design (4/9/13 baseline)

Requirement, as for the coupler: carry the running engine-side torque of 96 mN·m (1,375 W at 136,600 rpm, the cold-day 90% point) with a pull-out torque of at least twice that, 192 mN·m, with magnets at 120 °C. Gears leak more flux at their ends than couplers, so the 2-D torque is scaled by 0.75 here, against 0.85 for the coupler. The required magnet length follows from that.

Rotational stress

The engine hub's magnets reach a 9.4 mm radius, against 6.5 mm in the coupler hub, so they press on their sleeve much harder at 154,000 rpm. The sleeve has to be 1.1 mm thick, which is why the inner magnetic gap is 1.6 mm instead of 1.0 mm.

The sleeve needs about 75 µm of diametral interference, which is too much to press on, so wind it in place under tension. The outer rotor turns at less than half the engine speed, so its stress is low even though it is the largest part.

Losses at 139,000 rpm

A magnetic gear is not lossless the way a synchronous coupler is. The pole pieces see a field flipping at 9.3 kHz, and every magnet and back-iron sees the other rotor's chopped field passing at kilohertz rates. The estimates below come from the field model stepped through a full rotation cycle.

Mounting the rotors

  • Engine hub, 42 g on the compressor nut. This is ten times the mass of the stock front nut, and it is cantilevered ahead of the front bearing. Most of it is magnet (21.5 g), so lightening the core saves little. It changes the engine rotor's critical speeds and front-bearing load. JetCat or a rotordynamics check must clear it before anything spins.
  • Outer rotor, 77 g. On the Lehner's 5 mm shaft its first bending speed is about 64,000–96,000 rpm, on top of its 61,000–68,000 rpm operating range. It needs its own pair of bearings on the stationary housing, with the motor driven through a short shaft or a flexible coupling.
  • Stationary ring. The ring must stay concentric with two separately supported rotors inside 0.5 mm radial clearances, and react 0.65 N·m at pull-out. It is the hardest part to build accurately.

4. Face-to-face gear not feasible

The face gear was modelled ring by ring: each radius is unrolled into a flat strip, solved in 2-D, and the strips are summed. The layout is an engine disc, a flat ring of pole pieces 3.5 mm thick and a motor disc, with 0.6 mm axial gaps and 4 mm back-iron discs. To meet the torque requirement, the magnets have to reach 36 mm diameter.

The face layout fails for the same reason as the face coupler, made worse. Its torque comes from radius, so the magnets end up far out on the engine-speed disc, and nothing can hold them there at 154,000 rpm. It could only work if the engine-side disc turned much slower, which is the problem the gear was meant to solve.

The gear exchanges one hard problem, the motor at 1.7× its rated speed, for several moderate ones. The new problems are mass on the engine rotor, a third precision part, an extra bearing set and 7% loss. It becomes the better choice if the Lehner overspeed test fails or Lehner will not support 140,000 rpm, and only if JetCat's rotor can carry the hub.

6. Effects on the rest of the system

  • Generator voltage. At about 62,000 rpm the 1535/17 (Kv 1441) makes about 43 V open circuit. The 1,200 W design load then needs about 30 A DC at roughly 40 V, against about 12 A at 97 V for the direct coupler, so copper and rectifier losses rise. A higher-turn 1535 winding with Kv near 800 would restore about 75 V; check what Lehner offers.
  • Starting. Through the gear the motor turns at 19,600 rpm when the engine reaches its 44,000 rpm idle. It needs 2.25 times the cranking torque, about 70–135 mN·m, or 10–20 A. Slipping a gear pole during a hard start is less likely than with the coupler, because the gear's motor-side pull-out is 446 mN·m.
  • Max thrust. The gear stays engaged. If the load bank stays in constant-resistance mode, power rises with speed squared, to about 1.7 kW of shaft power at 154,000 rpm. That is 236 mN·m on the motor side, still well below pull-out. Alternatively, open the load during the hold before acceleration.
  • Score. The gear, its bearings and the ring weigh roughly 150–170 g, against about 75 g for the coupler plus its actuator. That is about 1–2 points at the weight score's 7.5 points per lbf. The axial package grows by about 10–16 mm, about 1–2 points on length.
  • Engine cycle. The extra 95 W of loss is extracted from the engine like generator power, so cover it in the cycle analysis.

7. Next steps if the gear is pursued

  1. Ask JetCat about mass at the nut. Can the P100 rotor carry about 42 g overhung on the compressor nut? If not, stop here.
  2. Confirm the 2-D model in FEMM for the 4/9/13 baseline, with non-linear SMC and steel curves, then estimate the 3-D end loss for the 20 mm length.
  3. Get SMC loss data near 10 kHz from Höganäs, or measure a ring sample. That figure drives the efficiency estimate.
  4. Lay out the housing. Outer-rotor bearings, the ring mount on the engine front case, and the concentricity stack-up for the two 0.5 mm gaps.
  5. Re-run the cycle analysis with about 1,470 W of shaft extraction and check thrust at 90% and EGT.

Model notes and assumptions

  • Field model. 2-D finite-volume solution for magnetic vector potential on a polar grid (coaxial) or on unrolled flat strips (face, six radii summed). Magnets are treated with relative permeability 1.0 and iron is linear (back-iron μr 1000, SMC μr 500), so saturation is handled by rejecting designs over 1.4 T in the pole pieces or 1.5 T in the back-irons. Torque comes from Maxwell stress averaged over each air gap.
  • Validation. A flat strip reproduces the closed-form coupler shear stress to 0.1%. On the full polar model the direct coupler comes out 5% below the closed form, which ignores curvature: about 229 mN·m instead of 242 mN·m at 120 °C after the 0.85 factor, still above the 192 mN·m requirement. Gear torque ratios match pl/ph within 1–3%.
  • Coupler cup flux. The polar model shows the direct coupler's 1.5 mm cup at about 1.9 T at the 95th percentile, more than the 1.3 T fundamental-only estimate. A 2.0 mm wall brings it to 1.5 T.
  • End effects. 2-D torque is multiplied by 0.75 for the coaxial gear and 0.8 for the face gear. These are assumed values until a 3-D model or bench test confirms them.
  • Loads. Load cases, the 2× pull-out factor and the 120 °C magnet temperature are the same as in the coupler sizing. Engine speeds are 90% corrected (139,000 rpm at 15 °C), with maximum 154,000 rpm and proof 1.15× maximum.
  • Losses. Somaloy 700HR 3P losses are fitted to the datasheet's 1 and 2 kHz points and extrapolated, with a 1.3 factor for harmonics. Magnet and lamination eddy losses use a resistance-limited thin-plate estimate from the field swing in each rotor's frame. These are first estimates, not measurements.
  • Materials. SmCo 30H (Br 1.08 T at 20 °C, −0.035 %/K), CFRP sleeve allowable 1,000 MPa, Somaloy density 7,520 kg/m³. Masses assume solid rings; the hub is a steel back-iron ring on a titanium hub.
